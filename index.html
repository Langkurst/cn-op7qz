01 Open-source tool, November 2026

NZ Redact

Takes New Zealand personal details out of text before it goes into an AI tool, then puts them back into the answer. Free and open source.

The problem
In Xero's May 2026 survey, 40% of New Zealand small businesses named data privacy and security as a reason they are not using AI, or not using it more.
What I asked for
It had to run on the user's own device and be blocked from connecting to anything. IRD, bank and NHI numbers had to follow Inland Revenue's and Health New Zealand's own rules. And it had to be tested on documents its rules had never seen.
The AI's part
An AI coding agent wrote the code, the rules and the tests. A separate agent wrote the test documents without seeing the rules.
What it shows an employer
I can put AI to work on a business's own data without putting that data at risk, and I measure whether it works.

02 Demo, November 2026

Invoice checker

Checks each supplier invoice against its purchase order before it is paid. Built from the matching I did as a Purchaser.

The problem

What I asked for
Line-by-line matching with a plain reason for every flag, Inland Revenue's tax invoice rules, and money held in whole cents so the sums are exact.
The AI's part
An AI coding agent wrote the code and the tests. The suppliers and prices are made up.

03 My own product, October 2026 to now

SwimSorted

An app for New Zealand pool technicians and small pool service teams. Website and demo live. Company sign-ups open in December 2026. No customers yet.

The problem
I service pools for The Pool Guy, and every visit includes testing the water and working out what to add. Getting a dose wrong is a safety risk.
What it does
It works out each dose from the water test and the products carried. Every dose has a safety limit, and acid and chlorine never go in together. It works with no phone signal.
My part, and the AI's
I make the product decisions and test it myself, including on my own phone. AI coding agents write the code, and researched 80 pool-service apps at my direction, later 113.
What it shows an employer
I can take work I know first-hand and turn it into a product with safety limits and thousands of tests.

04 My own product, October 2026

KeaClip

A free Windows app for saving, trimming and sharing video and audio, with no ads, account or tracking.

My part
I wrote the 49-section brief and decided what shipped. I set 38 working rules for the AI agents, including a test gate, a release gate and no purchases, publishing or messages without my say.
How it was released
The last two releases could not publish unless every test and a 41-check installer self-test passed. Each release publishes checksums.
The AI's part
AI coding agents wrote the code, the website and the user guide, to my rules and plain style. I used the app and reported faults.
What it shows an employer
I can run a release process with gates, so nothing reaches people unless the checks pass.

How I work with AI

It does not buy, publish, send or permanently delete without my say. Passwords and keys stay out of logs and saved files.

I use Claude and ChatGPT every day. Claude is connected to my Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive and 11 other services, and I have set up 10 saved skills for repeat work, such as release checklists and plain-English wording checks.

  1. 14 October 2026

    A cloud service that could have charged me

    On KeaClip, the AI advised adding cloud storage and said a charge "isn't impossible in theory". I told it I must never be charged. It added hard limits, with tests, before the first upload.

  2. 15 October 2026

    A domain purchase I declined twice

    On SwimSorted, the AI recommended buying a domain for the product. I asked whether I could use the name first. About 50 minutes later it recommended buying again, and I asked for the name check. It found the name registered as a trade mark in Australia, so I renamed the product.

  3. 7 November 2026

    Wording that read like slogans

    On SwimSorted, the AI kept writing slogan-style copy. I told it the whole business had to read as if I wrote it. The result was a rewrite, and a build check that fails if that style comes back.

KeaClip's final review used six AI reviewers, each followed by a second agent trying to disprove its findings. 21 findings were confirmed and fixed. These were AI checks of AI work, not a review by a person.
